Why this exists

MEV bots don't announce themselves. Their trades just look wrong.

A sandwich attacker's front-run pushes the pool's price away from the truth. A JIT bot's liquidity shows up and leaves inside one block. Neither has to say what it's doing — the shape of the trade already gives it away, if something is watching for it.

Signal 1 — is this trade honest with the outside world?

Compares the pool's own price against a trusted Chainlink reading. A swap that widens the gap is taxed. A swap that closes it is discounted.

deviationBps = |pool − oracle| / oracle
score = min(deviationBps, cap) / cap

Signal 2 — is this trade honest with this pool's own history?

Compares this swap's price impact against a running average of what's normal for this specific pool — no fixed threshold, self-calibrating.

excessRatio = impact / baseline(EMA)
score = min(excessRatio / mult, 1)

Combined — a noisy-OR, not an average

An earlier version averaged the two scores — and testing found a real bug: it diluted a maximally-confident signal by half. Fixed to noisy-OR, verified with exact numbers before and after.

combined = 1 − (1 − s1)(1 − s2) fee = BASE + combined × (MAX − BASE)

The problem: value that leaves without asking

A liquidity provider deposits into a pool expecting to earn fees. What isn't obvious is how much of that fee income is quietly handed straight back out to two kinds of traders who never had to reveal what they were doing. The first is the sandwich attacker, who pushes the pool's price away from the true market price on purpose, trades against the LP at the distorted price, then lets an honest arbitrageur correct it back — collecting the difference. The second is the JIT (just-in-time) liquidity bot, which adds a large, precisely-ranged position moments before a known large swap, captures a disproportionate share of that single swap's fee, and withdraws immediately after — inside one block, with almost no price risk taken on at all.

Neither attacker has to announce their intent. A sandwich attack looks, on the surface, like three ordinary swaps. A JIT position looks like ordinary liquidity provision. What gives both away is the shape of the activity — if something is actually watching for it.

The two signals

Signal 1 asks a simple question: does this swap push the pool's price further from, or closer to, a trusted outside reading? It compares the pool's own price against Chainlink's ETH/USD feed. A swap that widens that gap is charged more; a swap that closes it is charged less.

deviationBps = |pool − oracle| / oracle score1 = min(deviationBps, cap) / cap

Signal 2 asks a different question: is this swap unusually large for this specific pool, right now? Rather than a fixed size threshold — which would need constant recalibration as a pool's real volume changes — it compares the swap's actual price impact against a running exponential moving average of what's structurally normal for that pool.

excessRatio = impact / baseline(EMA) score2 = min(excessRatio / mult, 1)

A real bug, found and fixed: the original design averaged the two scores together. Real testing showed this quietly diluted a maximally-confident single signal by roughly half — a swap that Signal 1 alone would have flagged at full severity got cut down simply because Signal 2 had nothing to add. Replaced with a noisy-OR combination, which correctly treats either signal alone as sufficient grounds for a full response.

combined = 1 − (1 − s1)(1 − s2) fee = BASE + combined × (MAX_SURCHARGE − BASE)

Theoretical grounding: both signals independently correspond to recognized loss-versus-rebalancing (LVR) mitigation strategies described in the academic literature (Milionis, Moallemi, Roughgarden, Zhang, 2022) — arrived at through iteration and testing, not derived from the papers, but genuinely validated by them after the fact. Full derivation and citation in the repository's MATH.md.

JIT liquidity defense: taxing the drive-by

Neither signal above ever sees a JIT attack, because neither one watches liquidity events at all — they only run during a swap. JIT liquidity needs its own, entirely separate defense.

The mechanism tracks the exact block a position was added, keyed to the specific pool, owner, tick range, and salt. When that same position is removed, it checks how many blocks it was actually held for. If liquidity was added and removed within the same block — the textbook JIT signature — 80% of the fees that position earned (never its deposited principal) are redirected to the pool's honest, long-term LPs instead. That penalty decays linearly, reaching 0% by the 10th block held.

blocksHeld = block.number − liquidityAddedAtBlock[positionKey] penaltyBps = JIT_MAX_PENALTY_BPS × (JIT_DECAY_BLOCKS − blocksHeld) / JIT_DECAY_BLOCKS

Why a decay, not a hard cutoff: an earlier version simply checked "same block, yes or no." That has a real, exploitable weakness — a bot patient enough to wait exactly one extra block pays nothing at all. A smooth decay removes that cliff entirely.

Verified exact, not approximate: a position removed at precisely the halfway point (5 of 10 blocks) produced a penalty of exactly half the same-block value — 11,999,999,999,999,999 wei versus 23,999,999,999,999,999 wei. Verified live on Sepolia: a real position held for 2 real blocks produced a real, correctly-scaled, nonzero penalty, confirmed both by the emitted event and by a persisting on-chain reserve balance afterward.

Oracle safety: three independent layers

Signal 1's entire value depends on one assumption: that the oracle it reads from is telling the truth. Three separate, layered defenses protect that single dependency, so that no one of them failing is catastrophic on its own.

Layer 1 — Timelock

A 24-hour delay on every oracle and guardian change. Always on, with no dependency on any external system — the simplest, most reliable layer, by design.

Layer 2 — OracleGuardian

A Reactive Smart Contract, deployed on Reactive Network's Lasna testnet, subscribes to oracle-change events emitted on Sepolia. When one fires, the contract reacts entirely autonomously — no off-chain bot, no server, no keeper — and triggers a cross-chain callback that pauses the pool. Proven live: a real, queued oracle change produced a real, confirmed pause in a single block, roughly 12 seconds end to end.

Layer 3 — ZKPriceGuardian

Even a real, honest Chainlink feed is still one single source. ZKPriceGuardian checks it against an independent, cryptographically-verified reading obtained via Reclaim Protocol's zkTLS — proving a real HTTPS response actually occurred, without needing to trust whoever relays that proof. If the two sources diverge by 5% or more, the pool pauses. Proven live: a real proof attesting $2422.80 was generated, submitted on-chain, and checked directly against Chainlink's own live reading in the same transaction.

Real bugs found, not hypothetical ones

The guardian-swap timelock vulnerability. On a final, deliberate review of every externally-callable function, we found that changing an already-active guardian had no timelock at all — the only trust-affecting change in the entire contract without one. A compromised configurer could have instantly disabled both OracleGuardian and ZKPriceGuardian, silently, before ever attempting a malicious oracle change — meaning neither safety layer would ever get a chance to fire. We wrote a test that first demonstrated this exact exploit and confirmed it passed, proving the vulnerability was real, then applied the same 24-hour timelock pattern already proven for oracle changes, and confirmed the identical test then correctly failed — direct, before-and-after evidence that the fix genuinely works, not just an assertion that it should.

The missing receive() for native ETH. Every one of this project's 100+ local tests used two ERC20 tokens for its pool currencies — never native ETH. That meant a real, production-breaking bug went completely undetected: the hook had no way to accept an incoming ETH transfer at all, so any skim (whether from the swap fee mechanism or the JIT penalty) that needed to deliver native ETH to the contract would simply revert. This was only found because we insisted on a real, live JIT demonstration against a real currency0=ETH pool on Sepolia, and it reverted. Fixed with a minimal receive() external payable {}, then closed permanently with three new, dedicated native-ETH tests, and confirmed working with a successful live re-run immediately after.
